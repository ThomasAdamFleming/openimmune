Why this matters · 05 · Multiple sclerosis in particular

Neurology

Multiple sclerosis in particular: a clear efficacy-erosion story that maps directly onto anti-drug antibodies.

05
Why this matters

Acting before the damage is done

For many people living with relapsing multiple sclerosis, a regular injection or infusion keeps the disease steady: fewer relapses, and less of the disability that can otherwise build up year on year. In some patients, though, the immune system slowly produces antibodies that cancel the drug out, so it quietly stops protecting them. The warning tends to come late, when relapses return or a scan shows new activity, by which point function may have been lost that cannot be regained.

Because time counts for so much in MS, choosing a treatment that will keep working is far from trivial. Predicting who is likely to develop these antibodies, and against which drug, would let neurologists pick an effective therapy from the outset and act before the damage is done.

What is happening

Antibodies that quietly cancel a drug out

Neutralising antibodies to interferon-beta reduce the drug's biological activity and, with it, its efficacy. Antibodies to natalizumab drive infusion reactions and loss of response. Both are chronic, repeat-dosing settings, again the conditions under which immunogenicity matters most.

The ordering of risk across products is itself instructive, because it tracks the molecule's sequence and how it is made, with a person's own immune-recognition genes adding risk on top.

The evidence

Well documented, well quantified

  • <1% neutralising antibodies with a pegylated interferon, against 28 to 47 per cent for an older subcutaneous formulation: immunogenicity tracks the molecule's sequence and format. Filipi & Jack, Int J MS Care 2019
  • 46% of persistently antibody-positive patients had hypersensitivity reactions on one therapy, against 0.7 per cent of antibody-negative patients, alongside loss of efficacy. Calabresi et al., Neurology 2007
  • 2.9M people live with MS worldwide, around 85 per cent with the relapsing form this most affects, including more than 150,000 in the UK and about a million in the US. Hittle et al., JAMA Neurol 2023
Where it applies

A defined and well-supported population

Principally relapsing multiple sclerosis, treated with chronic, repeat-dosing therapies.

  • Relapsing multiple sclerosis
  • Interferon-beta therapies
  • Natalizumab
  • Anti-CD20 agents

Newer anti-CD20 agents sit at the low end for immunogenicity, while the older interferons span a wide range, an ordering that a sequence-aware model is well placed to explain and predict.

Because these antibody problems are so well documented, the value of predicting them is unusually concrete: an effective therapy chosen from the outset, and action before function is lost.
