The problem

Powerful medicines, an unpredictable response

Biologic medicines are among the most effective treatments in modern medicine. For many people they are the thing that finally controls a disease nothing else could touch. And then, for a significant number of those people, the medicine quietly stops working. Not because the disease has changed, but because their own immune system has learned to recognise the drug as foreign and to attack it.

This is immunogenicity: the tendency of a biologic medicine to provoke the immune system against itself. It is one of the largest unsolved problems in medicine, and we still cannot reliably predict it. The mechanism is understood; the data needed to see it coming has never been built.

When a treatment fails this way, patients lose a medicine they often waited years for, drug developers lose work that reached the clinic, and the same problem is met again, in private, by the next team. The tools that do exist work least well for the patients already least well served, because they were built from a narrow slice of human biology.

Why it matters

A common harm, and a fixable one

This is not a rare event at the edge of medicine. It happens quietly, to many people, and it can be put right.

00

The treatment that never arrives

Many biologic medicines fail late in development, and the immune response is one of the leading reasons. Behind that is a simple fact: a medicine that fails this way is one that patients waited years for and will now never receive.

01

The treatment that is taken away

A significant share of people on long-term biologic therapy slowly lose the response, as the immune system learns to recognise the drug and attack it. These are people the medicine was working for. They then cycle through alternatives, each carrying the same risk, often getting worse in between.

02

An unfairness built into the tools

The prediction tools that exist today were built from a narrow slice of human biology. The part of us that governs this response varies enormously between populations, so the tools work least well for the patients already least well served by medicine. A dataset built deliberately across that diversity is the way to put it right.

The objective

One general way to predict immune response

The goal is an open source, generalisable model that predicts the immunogenicity of a biologic medicine from its sequence: a single, general method, not a closed tool built for one drug or one company.

To train it, OpenImmune is building a multimodal dataset of the immune response at a scale and breadth that has not existed before. The dataset and the model are the lasting outcomes, and both will be released openly so the whole field can rely on them. The aim is to move biologic development from reacting to immune responses to predicting them.

The solution

A build no one would do alone

No organisation alone can create this. The OpenImmune consortium brings the disciplines together and releases the result.

An unprecedented dataset

An open dataset of the immune response at a scale and breadth that has not existed before, bringing the different layers of the biology together in one place. The enabling foundation that prediction has always lacked.

A coalition of capabilities

A problem this large needs more than one discipline. OpenImmune brings together autonomous experimentation, immune sequencing, artificial intelligence, clinical insight, and open-data stewardship, together with academia, pharma, biotech and techbio, in a coalition of a kind not assembled before.

Open by design

The dataset and the models built on it are released openly as a public good, for the whole ecosystem to build on. A single build, shared, so the field moves forward together.

Why it is funded this way

Built as a public good, funded as one

Shared, open datasets and models like this will not be built by the market. No single company can justify building them and then giving it away, even though all of them need it. That is the gap OpenImmune exists to close.

So the work is funded neutrally, through government and philanthropy rather than any one company. Neutral funding is what keeps the result open, general, and in the public interest. Neutral money builds the commons; commercial money builds on it.

This is the first, foundational phase. It is built to prove what is possible and to set the ground for a far larger effort to follow, one that could change how biologic medicines are designed and tested, and help more patients keep the treatments that work.
