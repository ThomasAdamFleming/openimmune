Why this matters · 06 · Population scale

Large-population secondary areas

Four further areas where an immune reaction is uncommon per person, but adds up at the scale of tens or hundreds of millions treated.

06

In eye disease, severe asthma and eczema, cardiovascular prevention and diabetes, an immune reaction to a biologic is uncommon for any one person. At the scale of hundreds of millions treated, uncommon still means a great many people. OpenImmune is building the open capability to predict who is at risk.

Why this matters

Keeping the serious reactions rare

These are conditions treated on an enormous scale: sight-threatening eye disease, severe asthma and eczema, cardiovascular prevention, and diabetes. For most people on these medicines, a reaction to the drug is uncommon and causes no harm. But when tens or hundreds of millions of people are treated, even a small rate adds up to a large number who lose vision, lose control of their condition, or suffer an inflammatory reaction that need not have happened.

Insulin tells the encouraging half of the story: once it was understood why people reacted to the early animal insulins, and the sequence was matched to human insulin, a once-common problem all but disappeared. Predicting immune reactions would help keep the serious ones rare, and guide the design of medicines that are given to much of the world.

The evidence

Four areas, one pattern

In each, an immune reaction is uncommon per person, but the population treated is so large that even a small rate is a substantial number of people.

Ophthalmology · anti-VEGF therapySight-threatening eye disease

Wet age-related macular degeneration and diabetic macular oedema are treated with anti-VEGF biologics across very large populations. For most agents, per-patient antibody rates are modest, around 1 to 10 per cent, and clinically quiet. The exception proves the mechanistic point: one small single-chain fragment drew treatment-emergent antibodies in about 75 per cent of patients, with intraocular inflammation in around 4.6 per cent. Immunogenicity that tracks the molecular format is exactly what a sequence model sets out to predict. Kim & Woo 2024

Respiratory and atopic diseaseSevere asthma and eczema

Biologics for severe asthma and atopic dermatitis serve large and growing populations in chronic, repeat-dosing use. Pooled anti-drug antibody rates are low, around 2.9 per cent across the class, but vary by molecule from near-zero to roughly 8 per cent. A smaller but real factor as post-market data accumulate. Chen et al., JACI Pract 2023

Cardiovascular prevention · PCSK9 inhibitorsLong-term prevention at scale

Given for long-term prevention across enormous populations. Antibodies to the fully human agents are low and clinically silent. The cautionary case is a humanised agent withdrawn from development after about 44 per cent of patients developed anti-drug antibodies, with progressive loss of effect: a clean demonstration that how human a biologic's sequence is governs its immune fate. PMC6790354

Endocrine · insulin · the solved case

Insulin immunogenicity was historically significant: anti-drug antibodies in roughly 25 to 30 per cent of patients on early bovine insulin. It fell to 10 to 15 per cent with porcine insulin, and to under 5 per cent with recombinant human insulin, tracking the reduction in sequence differences from human insulin, three, then one, then none. It is the canonical worked example of a solved immunogenicity problem, and of the sequence-to-immunogenicity relationship itself.

Where it applies

Given to much of the world

Medicines given at a scale where a low per-patient rate still matters cumulatively.

  • Wet AMD and diabetic macular oedema
  • Severe asthma and atopic dermatitis
  • Cardiovascular prevention
  • Type 2 diabetes

Familial hypercholesterolaemia alone affects around 34 million people, wet AMD around 20 million, and the treated population for type 2 diabetes now exceeds 500 million.

None of these is a crisis per patient. But at this scale, predicting the rare serious reactions, and guiding the design of medicines given to much of the world, is clearly worth doing.
