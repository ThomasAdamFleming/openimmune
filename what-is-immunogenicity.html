Background

What is immunogenicity?

The plain version of the problem OpenImmune exists to solve, written for anyone who needs to understand it and has never had it explained clearly.

The short answer

A medicine the body decides to treat as foreign

Immunogenicity is the tendency of a medicine to provoke the patient's own immune system against it. It matters most for biologic medicines, which are made of proteins rather than small chemical molecules. The immune system is built to recognise foreign proteins and remove them, and it does not reliably distinguish between a protein from a virus and a protein that arrived in an infusion bag.

When that happens, the body produces anti-drug antibodies: antibodies aimed at the treatment itself. The medicine is cleared faster, or blocked outright, and a treatment that was working stops working.

Why it happens

Nothing has gone wrong; the immune system is doing its job

It helps to say plainly that immunogenicity is not a manufacturing defect or a mistake in how a medicine was given. It is the immune system performing its ordinary function on an unfamiliar protein.

Whether that happens depends on two things at once. The first is the medicine: its sequence, its structure, and how far it differs from proteins the body already knows. The second is the patient: the inherited genetics of immune recognition vary a great deal between people and between populations, so the same medicine can be tolerated indefinitely by one person and rejected by another.

That second half is what makes the problem genuinely hard. There is no single answer for a medicine, only an answer for a medicine in a person.

What it costs

Two ways a treatment is lost

A treatment can be lost before it exists. Many biologic candidates fail during development, and an unwanted immune response is one of the leading reasons a promising one is abandoned late. Every medicine lost this way is one that patients waited years for and will now never receive.

A treatment can also be lost after it arrives. People on long-term biologic therapy can slowly lose the benefit as their immune system learns to neutralise the drug, and then begin cycling through alternatives that each carry the same risk, often getting worse in between.

There is a third cost that is easy to miss. The prediction tools that exist today were built largely from European genetic data, while the biology that governs immune recognition varies enormously between populations. The tools work least well for the people already least well served.

Why it is hard to predict

The barrier is not the algorithm; it is the data

The mechanism is understood in outline. What has never existed is a body of measurement large enough, broad enough, and consistent enough to train a general model on.

The data that does exist sits in fragments: inside individual companies, tied to individual drug programmes, generated by different methods that cannot be compared, and rarely published. Each team meets the problem in private and solves a small piece of it for one molecule. The field has never had one open, coherent foundation to build on.

This is why the same problem is met again and again. It is a structural gap rather than a scientific mystery, and structural gaps can be closed.

What changes

From reacting to immunogenicity to predicting it

If the response could be predicted from a sequence before anyone is treated, three things change. Developers could design out the risk while a medicine is still on the bench, rather than discovering it in the clinic. Clinicians could choose the treatment most likely to hold for the person in front of them, from the first dose. And regulators and researchers would share a common, open reference rather than a patchwork of private results.

That is what OpenImmune is building: an open, foundational dataset of the immune response, and a generalisable model trained on it, both released openly under FAIR principles for anyone to use. Eight areas show what it would mean in practice.
