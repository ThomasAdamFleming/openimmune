Why this matters · 03 · Enzyme replacement and gene therapy

Rare and paediatric disease

Enzyme replacement, storage disorders and gene therapy: the area of greatest humanitarian weight, where an immune reaction can decide whether a child's treatment works at all.

03
Why this matters

Guarding against a reaction from the first dose

For a child born with a rare disorder such as Pompe, Fabry or one of the MPS conditions, a manufactured enzyme, or increasingly a gene therapy, can be the difference between a full life and an early decline. The obstacle is that these children's bodies have often never made the protein in question, so the immune system can treat the life-changing therapy as an intruder and cancel out its effect. In the most severe cases in infancy, that immune reaction is one of the main things that decides whether the treatment works at all.

Gene therapy faces a related barrier: many patients already carry antibodies to the delivery vehicle and are turned away before they can be treated. Predicting these reactions ahead of time would let doctors guard against them from the first dose, and open safe treatment to children who are currently excluded. For families with few other options, that matters enormously.

What is happening

A therapy the immune system reads as foreign

Enzyme replacement delivers a protein to children who may have little or none of it natively, so the immune system can treat the therapy as foreign and raise antibodies that blunt or abolish its effect. This is starkest in the most severe infantile cases, where high, sustained antibody levels can decide the outcome.

Gene therapy adds two further barriers of intense current interest: pre-existing antibodies to the delivery vehicle, which can exclude patients from treatment altogether, and immune responses to the therapeutic protein once it is expressed.

The evidence

Where a reaction decides the outcome

  • 27 months. On enzyme replacement alone, essentially all infants of the most severe subtype had died or become ventilator-dependent by this age, before immune tolerance approaches were used. Kishnani et al., Mol Genet Metab 2009
  • 74 to 100% survival in those same infants when the immune response is suppressed first with a tolerance-induction regimen: the difference between life and death turns on managing immunogenicity. Li et al., 2021
  • 40 to 70% of treated males in Fabry disease develop anti-drug antibodies, associated with a measurable loss of kidney function over time. Lenders et al., JASN 2016
Where it applies

Individually rare, collectively significant

Conditions that are individually rare, collectively significant, and heavily weighted towards children.

  • Pompe disease
  • Fabry disease
  • MPS I, II and VI
  • Gene-therapy recipients

In some storage disorders almost every treated patient seroconverts, and a large share of adults carry pre-existing antibodies to common gene-therapy delivery vehicles, enough to exclude many otherwise eligible candidates. Per-patient costs are among the highest in medicine.

In this repeat-dosing, antibody-driven setting, an immune reaction can determine whether a therapy works at all. Predicting it ahead of time would open safe treatment to children who are currently turned away.
