Haemophilia and bleeding disorders

The cleanest, best-characterised immunogenicity problem in medicine, and one of the most consequential.

02
Why this matters

The safest option, from the very first treatment

People with severe haemophilia depend on infusions of clotting factor to stop bleeds that would otherwise be dangerous. For a sizeable minority, the immune system turns on the very protein meant to protect them, producing what doctors call inhibitors: antibodies that switch the treatment off. Almost overnight, a manageable condition becomes dangerous and far more expensive to treat, bleeds grow hard to control, and clearing the inhibitor takes many months of demanding, uncertain therapy.

Because this reaction usually appears early in life and cannot yet be predicted, families and clinicians often discover it only once it has already happened. Flagging the risk in advance, from a child's own genetics and the make-up of the product, would let doctors choose the safest option from the very first treatment, and avoid the most feared complication in haemophilia care.

What is happening

A protein the body never learned to tolerate

Factor replacement introduces a protein that a severely deficient person's immune system has, in effect, never learned to tolerate. A substantial minority develop neutralising inhibitors, with previously untreated patients at highest risk.

The risk tracks the underlying mutation, exactly the sequence-level signal a predictive model targets, with a person's own immune-recognition genes adding further risk on top.

The evidence

The most consequential immunogenicity problem there is

  • 44.5% cumulative inhibitor incidence with recombinant factor VIII in a landmark randomised trial, against 26.8 per cent with plasma-derived factor: the product itself changes the odds. Peyvandi et al., NEJM 2016
  • 3.6× the higher inhibitor risk carried by large gene deletions compared with the common intron-22 inversion, a clear demonstration that the mutation drives the immune response. Gouw et al., J Thromb Haemost 2012
  • 30.8% overall inhibitor incidence in a large real-world cohort of previously untreated patients, concentrated in the first 10 to 20 exposure days. Gouw et al., NEJM 2013
  • 3 to 6× how much more it costs to manage a patient once inhibitors develop, before counting the years of demanding therapy needed to try to clear one. J Manag Care Spec Pharm 2023
Where it applies

Smaller in number, defined by severity and cost

A smaller population than the inflammatory diseases, but one marked by high severity and very high cost per patient.

  • Severe haemophilia A
  • Haemophilia B
  • Other replacement-factor disorders

A global survey identified 179,703 people with haemophilia A and 37,385 with haemophilia B, against an estimated 836,000 expected worldwide (WFH Annual Global Survey 2023), so most people who could be helped are not yet reached.

Inhibitor development is the archetypal biologic immunogenicity problem, defined tightly enough that a predictive tool has an obvious, quantifiable value: the safest product chosen from the first dose.
