Why this matters

The immune response decides who a medicine can help

Some of the most powerful medicines we have can fail when the body turns against them. These are the areas where predicting that response, before the first dose, would change lives, and the open capability that would make it possible.

The human case

A common, fixable harm, distributed unfairly

The treatment that never arrives

Lost before it is licensed

Roughly nine in ten biologic candidates fail before they reach patients, and an immune reaction to the medicine is a leading cause of late-stage failure. Every drug lost this way is a treatment people waited years for, and will now never receive.

The treatment that is taken away

Working, then not

For a significant share of patients on long-term biologic therapy, a medicine that initially worked simply stops working, because the immune system has learned to neutralise it. Then begins a costly cycling process to seek for alternatives, each carrying the same risk, often with worsening disease in between.

An unfairness in the tools

Built for some, not all

The prediction tools that exist today were built mostly from European genetic data, yet the biology that governs the immune response varies enormously between populations. So the tools work least well for the people already least well served. Building openly, across global diversity, is the rectifying step needed.

We can already make medicines that give people their lives back. The open question is whose body will let them keep it.

Eight ways it matters

Where the immune response decides the outcome

One capability, applied across the field. It begins with the science the whole programme depends on, reaches into the areas where it counts most, and turns, at the end, into a tool for building immune responses rather than avoiding them.

00

The AI moonshot: predicting immunogenicity from sequence

Not a disease, but the general capability the whole programme exists to create. AlphaFold showed that a protein's shape could be predicted from its sequence, and then given to everyone. Predicting whether the immune system will react to a biologic is a problem of similar depth, and it has not yet had its breakthrough. The blocker has never been the algorithms; it has been open data at the right scale.

Applications across disease
01

Immune-mediated inflammatory disease

The largest and most direct application. For millions with rheumatoid arthritis, Crohn's, colitis or psoriasis, a biologic is what makes an ordinary life possible, until, for many, it quietly stops working.

Explore 02

Haemophilia and bleeding disorders

The cleanest, best-characterised therapeutic-protein immunogenicity problem in medicine. For a sizeable minority, the immune system turns on the very protein meant to protect them.

Explore 03

Rare and paediatric disease

The area of greatest humanitarian weight, where an immune reaction to a manufactured enzyme, or a gene therapy, can decide whether a child's treatment works at all.

Explore 04

Cell and gene therapy in oncology

The frontier of cell therapy, where durability is everything. When the immune system clears an engineered therapy too soon, the cancer can return.

Explore 05

Neurology and multiple sclerosis

A clear efficacy-erosion story. In some people the immune system slowly cancels out a drug that was holding the disease steady, and the warning comes late.

Large-population secondary areas

Eye disease, severe asthma and eczema, cardiovascular prevention, and diabetes. A small reaction rate still means a large number of people when hundreds of millions are treated.

The inverted case
07

Vaccines: immunogenicity by design

The mirror image of everything else here. In vaccines, an immune response to a foreign protein is exactly what we want. The same model, run the other way, becomes a tool for designing responses rather than suppressing them: vaccines that protect more people, and faster design against a new pathogen.

Why philanthropy, and why open

A public good the market will not build

A shared, open foundation like this will not be built by any single company. Its value is diffuse: it lowers the risk of every biologic, for every developer, which is precisely why no one company will build an open version and give it away. That is the space philanthropy exists to fill, to capitalise a commons that industry cannot, and to move a whole field rather than a single product.

The timing is real. The methods this depends on, immune-repertoire sequencing at scale, structural inference after AlphaFold, and autonomous laboratories, have only recently become available together. The reasons it has not been built are not scientific; they are structural, and they can be addressed.
