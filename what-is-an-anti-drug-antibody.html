Background

What is an anti-drug antibody?

The specific thing that goes wrong when a biologic medicine stops working, and the difference between an antibody that matters and one that does not.

The short answer

An antibody the body makes against the treatment

An anti-drug antibody, usually shortened to ADA, is an antibody produced by a patient's immune system against a medicine they have been given. It is the concrete form that immunogenicity takes.

Antibodies are the immune system's targeting mechanism. They are made to recognise a particular shape on a particular protein and mark it for removal. When the protein they recognise is a biologic medicine, the immune system starts removing the treatment.

The distinction that matters

Binding, neutralising, and the difference in consequence

Not every anti-drug antibody changes the outcome, and the distinction is worth holding onto.

A binding antibody attaches to the medicine somewhere that does not interfere with how it works. The main effect is that the drug is cleared from the body faster, so less of it is available for less time. A patient may notice nothing, or may need a higher or more frequent dose.

A neutralising antibody attaches at the part of the medicine that does the work, and blocks it. The drug may still be present in the blood and still do nothing at all. In some conditions this is the single most feared complication of treatment, and the reason a manageable disease can become a dangerous one.

What it looks like in practice

The medicine that quietly stops working

From the patient's side, an anti-drug antibody response rarely announces itself. A treatment that was controlling a disease becomes less effective over months. Symptoms return. The obvious first interpretation is that the disease has worsened, not that the medicine is being neutralised.

Because the response usually cannot be anticipated, it tends to be identified only after it has already cost the patient the treatment. What follows is a search for an alternative, which carries the same risk again, often with the disease worsening in between.

Why it is hard to anticipate

Same medicine, different person, different answer

Whether a person develops anti-drug antibodies depends on the medicine and on them. The sequence and structure of the drug set part of the risk. The rest sits in the patient's own immune-recognition genetics, in how and how often the medicine is given, and in the disease being treated.

No single one of those explains the outcome, and the combined effect is exactly the kind of pattern a model can learn, given enough consistent measurement across enough different people. That measurement is what has never been assembled openly, which is the gap OpenImmune exists to close.

Haemophilia is the clearest worked example, because the risk there is tightly enough characterised to be quantified. Multiple sclerosis shows the slow-erosion version of the same process.
