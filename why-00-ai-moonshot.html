The AI moonshot: predicting immunogenicity from sequence

Not a disease, but the general capability the whole programme exists to create: a way to tell, from a biologic's design, whether the immune system will react to it.

00
Why this matters

Answer the question once, in the open

Behind every other page here is a single question: will a person's body accept the medicine meant to help them? Today that question is answered slowly and expensively, one drug at a time, and sometimes only after someone has already reacted. This is the chance to answer it differently, and to answer it once.

AlphaFold showed that a problem biologists had worked on for fifty years, predicting a protein's shape from its sequence, could be largely solved by artificial intelligence and then given away, free, to millions of researchers. Predicting whether the immune system will react to a biologic is a problem of similar depth, and it has not yet had its breakthrough. Solving it in the open would remove one of the last great uncertainties in the design of medicines, and, like AlphaFold, it would belong to everyone.

The challenge

Harder than shape, in one specific way

AlphaFold answered one great sequence-to-function question: given a sequence, what shape does it fold into? Immunogenicity is the next question of comparable reach, and it is harder in one specific sense. It is not a property of the molecule alone, but of the molecule in a host: it depends on how a protein is processed, on presentation by highly variable immune-recognition molecules, and on each person's own immune repertoire.

The genes involved are the most variable in the human genome, so the risk is patient-specific rather than the same for everyone. And the part that matters most for anti-drug antibodies, the MHC Class II pathway that recruits helper T cells, is precisely the part that today's tools handle least well. That is what makes it a genuine frontier rather than an incremental step.

The evidence

The gap, in numbers

The science is public. The scale of what a solution would serve, and the size of the gap, are not in dispute.

  • 214M predicted protein structures in the open AlphaFold database, used by more than two million researchers, a demonstration of what open data plus machine learning can do for biology. AlphaFold DB, Nucleic Acids Res 2024
  • 2,000+ known forms of a single immune-recognition gene, HLA-DRB1, alone. This extreme diversity is why immune risk is patient-specific, and why a prediction has to account for a person's own genetics. PLOS Biology 2019
  • 24 human Class II alleles covered by the leading public epitope tools, against 173 for Class I, and at lower accuracy. The pathway that governs anti-drug antibodies is the least well served. Frontiers in Immunology 2017
  • $400bn+ the global biologics market this capability would serve, where an immune reaction is a principal cause of late-stage failure and a single late failure can cost tens of millions. Grand View Research
Why it is tractable now

The blocker was never the algorithms

AlphaFold did not succeed because the algorithms suddenly appeared. It succeeded because half a century of open structural data in the Protein Data Bank, together with a shared benchmark run every two years since 1994 (Protein Data Bank; CASP, predictioncenter.org), gave a modern model something to learn from and a way to prove it worked. Immunogenicity has had no equivalent: no comparable open record of immune outcomes, and no shared benchmark.

That is the gap OpenImmune sets out to close, by building the open dataset, and in time the shared benchmark, that many models can compete on. The methods this depends on, immune-repertoire sequencing at scale and structural inference after AlphaFold, have only recently matured together. The reasons the problem is unsolved are not scientific; they are structural, and they can be addressed.

The prize

Two payoffs, on two horizons

The immediate one is a general capability that lowers immune risk earlier and more cheaply across the whole biologics pipeline, the return that every other page in this section draws on.

The lasting one is scientific: a foundational model and an open benchmark that become permanent infrastructure for immunology and drug design, a contribution on the scale of AlphaFold to a problem of comparable importance that has not yet had its moment.
